Pediatric Heuristic Analysis

Designing and Owning an Original Heuristic Framework for a Pediatric Drug Label

A UX/UI case study on building a custom heuristic framework, grounded in linguistics and childhood development research, to evaluate a pediatric Instructions for Use (IFU) label for a confidential specialty pharmaceutical client under NDA.

Overview

Defining what "good" meant for a pediatric label before evaluating one.

The Ask

The client, a specialty pharmaceutical company whose identity is withheld under NDA, needed an expert review of the pediatric Instructions for Use (IFU) accompanying one of their products. Standard usability heuristics weren't built for regulated health-literacy documents written for children and caregivers, so the ask wasn't just to run an evaluation. It was to define what "good" even meant for this kind of material first.

My Role

UX Designer, April to July 2026. My responsibilities included:

•Researching, drafting, and owning a full heuristic framework built specifically for pediatric health-literacy material, rather than adapting an existing one
•Benchmarking the pediatric IFU against comparable labels to understand the existing landscape
•Conducting the heuristic evaluation against the finalized framework
•Validating the framework against established readability instruments
•Delivering findings that directly informed the IFU revision, which was handed off to feed a regulatory submission
The Challenge
•No standard heuristic set exists for pediatric health-literacy materials; most usability heuristics are built for software interfaces, not regulated health information written for children and their caregivers
•The label had to work for two audiences at once, the child and the caregiver, each at a different reading and comprehension level
•Findings needed to be rigorous and defensible enough to inform an actual regulatory submission, not just serve as design opinion
Approach

Building the framework, then grounding it in the literature.

Since no existing heuristic set fit a pediatric-specific health-literacy context, I built one, anchoring it in established, credible external sources, then layering in a linguistics and childhood-education lens.

Theoretical Foundations
•American Academy of Pediatrics: developmental readiness, caregiver involvement
•CDC Health Literacy Guidelines: plain language, actionability
•NIH Clear Communication guidelines: comprehension, chunking, visuals
•Early childhood education frameworks: Piaget's stages of cognitive development, Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development, and dual coding theory, grounding tone, reading level, and engagement in how children actually process information at different ages
Readability Validation

To keep the framework from being purely qualitative, I cross-checked it against established readability instruments: Flesch-Kincaid (the standard used to validate U.S. Army operating manuals since 1975, and by several states to keep insurance, legal, and financial documents at or below a 9th-grade reading level), SMOG (a method well-suited to health-literacy material, which I'd previously calculated by hand during my linguistics coursework), and the Spache formula and Lexile levels as secondary references.

Discover

Benchmarking the label landscape.

Before drafting a single heuristic, I benchmarked the existing pediatric label landscape, reviewing comparable pediatric IFUs to understand how other labels handled structure and instructions, target population and collection considerations, and language burden. This benchmarking became its own section of the final deliverable, giving the client a grounded before-picture rather than abstract critique.

Define

Eleven candidate heuristics, refined to eight.

With the landscape mapped, I drafted a full candidate set of eleven heuristics, each built out with the same structure: a plain-language definition, a checklist of specific criteria, a diagnostic question an evaluator could actually apply section by section, like "Could a child in the target age range understand this without explanation?" or "Does this sound safe and supportive, not cold or scary?", and the specific literature it drew from.

I then refined that set down to the eight heuristics that anchored the final evaluation:

•Environmentally Appropriate Language
•User Audience Clarity
•Necessity & Non-Threatening Tone
•Use of Actionable Instructions
•Progressive Structure by Age
•Cognitive Load Management
•Inclusive & Accessible Communication
•Literacy & Critical Clarity
Design

Applying the framework section by section.

With the framework finalized, I applied it directly to the pediatric IFU, evaluating the label section by section against each of the eight heuristics and documenting findings within a consistent reporting structure. This mirrored the same definition-criteria-question format the heuristics themselves were built on, so every finding was traceable back to a specific, sourced standard rather than a subjective note.

Deliverable

An IFU Expert Review deck.

The final output was an IFU Expert Review deck: a benchmarking comparison section, a heuristic evaluation section organized around the eight finalized heuristics, and an appendix documenting the full heuristic set, language considerations, and benchmark comparisons in detail.

The findings fed directly into the IFU revision, which was then handed off to support a regulatory submission. This meant the framework I built didn't just critique the existing label; it became the standard the revision was measured against.
